# Five Channel Directions

Research for a premium faceless YouTube channel built on cinematic AI production.

**Stated advantages this study optimises against:** Web3 and crypto knowledge ·
marketing and GTM strategy · internet-business literacy · AI tooling fluency ·
Claude + Higgsfield access · creative and brand judgement.

**Excluded by instruction:** generic AI-news content. Also excluded on my own
judgement: anything whose primary asset is speed of reaction, since that trades
directly against the cinematic production cycle these tools imply.

---

## How the scores work

A flat 1–10 across twelve criteria would let cheap wins cancel out expensive
losses. These are weighted to the six things you said to prioritise, plus two
that decide whether a channel survives its first year.

| Criterion | Weight | Why it carries this weight |
|---|---:|---|
| Curiosity pull | 15 | The click is the whole funnel. Nothing downstream matters without it. |
| Commercial audience & monetisation | 16 | Highest weight: a faceless channel with cinematic costs needs revenue per view, not raw views. |
| Visual potential | 15 | This is the leverage the tooling actually provides. |
| Research credibility & defensibility | 15 | The only moat once generation is commoditised. |
| Evergreen value | 12 | Determines whether the back catalogue compounds or decays. |
| Repeatability | 12 | A format you cannot run 50 times is a video, not a channel. |
| Competitive whitespace | 10 | Discounts directions where an established channel already owns the search. |
| Production feasibility | 5 | Low weight — you have the tools. It breaks ties. |

## Direction 1 — *THE LEDGER*
### Forensic reconstruction of financial crime, read off the chain

**Premise.** Every video answers one question in forensic detail: *where did the
money actually go?* Crypto heists, laundering typologies, exchange collapses,
sanctions evasion, state-sponsored theft — reconstructed minute by minute from
public blockchain data, court filings, and incident reports.

**1. Core audience.** 25–45, financially literate, majority male, high income.
Crypto-native traders and builders; fintech, compliance and security
professionals; the much larger crowd who does not hold crypto but finds heists
irresistible. Geographically concentrated in US, UK, Germany, Singapore, UAE —
all high-CPM markets.

**2. Viewer promise.** *You will see exactly how the money moved, and exactly how
they were caught — or how they got away.* Not opinion, not prediction. A ledger.

**3. Example video topics.**
- The 14 minutes that emptied Bybit — how $1.5B left in a single approved transaction
- What North Korea actually buys with stolen crypto
- The laundering pattern that keeps failing at the same step
- Anatomy of a bridge exploit: the flaw that keeps getting rebuilt
- The exchange that was insolvent for two years before anyone checked
- How investigators trace a coin through 10,000 addresses
- The heist where the thief gave it all back — and why
- Tornado Cash: the tool, the sanction, and the argument nobody settled
- The mixer that was a honeypot
- Why the biggest theft in history was signed by the victims themselves

**4. Why people would click.** Heist is the most durable curiosity structure in
non-fiction. A specific number and a specific unanswered question in the title
("$1.5 billion left in one signature. Nobody noticed for an hour.") converts
without needing a face or a personality.

**5. Why people would keep watching.** The forensic trail is inherently
serialised — each hop raises the next question. Retention comes from structure,
not from stimulation, which is what separates this from AI slop. The viewer is
following a chase they can verify themselves.

**6. Monetisation potential. High.** Finance sits in the top CPM band —
trade estimates put finance at roughly **$15–50 CPM / $10–25 RPM**, versus
$1–8 for entertainment. Treat those figures as directional: they come from
SEO-driven industry blogs, not from audited data. The durable revenue here is
sponsorship and a paid research tier, not AdSense.

**7. Sponsorship categories.** Blockchain analytics and compliance (Chainalysis,
TRM, Elliptic tier) · hardware wallets and custody · exchanges and brokerages ·
cybersecurity and endpoint security · identity and KYC · fraud detection ·
enterprise data tools · VPN and privacy (down-market, use sparingly) ·
B2B SaaS with a security posture. Strong CPM floor even in a crypto bear market
because the security and compliance buyers are counter-cyclical.

**8. Production difficulty. Medium-high.** Research is the cost centre, not
pixels. Every claim needs a transaction hash, a filing, or a named report.
Budget 60% of effort on the source pack and 40% on production.

**9. Visual-generation potential. High, with a discipline requirement.** Wallet
flow graphs, address clustering, exchange floors, cold storage rooms, server
halls, timestamped transaction overlays, dramatised reconstruction of a signing
ceremony. The failure mode is obvious and fatal — hooded hacker at a green
terminal. The visual bible must ban it explicitly.

**10. Risk of becoming repetitive AI content. Low-medium.** The research layer
is genuinely hard to mass-produce, which is exactly what YouTube's inauthentic
content policy (renamed from "repetitious content", effective 15 July 2025)
now discriminates on. AI tooling remains fully monetisable where the video
carries original human value; a channel whose value is a source pack is on the
right side of that line. The residual risk is visual sameness, not editorial
sameness.

**11. What would make it defensible.**
- **A proprietary incident database.** Every video adds structured rows —
  addresses, dates, amounts, actors, method, outcome. After 30 videos you own a
  research asset nobody can clone by watching your videos.
- **On-chain literacy as the barrier.** Reading a laundering graph is a real
  skill. It is your stated advantage and it does not transfer by imitation.
- **A published correction policy.** Rigor is the brand. Visible corrections
  compound trust faster than being right quietly.
- **Named-source citation on screen.** Turns credibility into a visible format
  feature competitors would have to copy wholesale.

**12. Ten competitor and reference channels.**

|---|---|---|
| Coffeezilla | Direct | The rigor benchmark for crypto fraud. Face-led — your whitespace is faceless and cinematic. |
| MagnatesMedia | Direct | ~1.5M subs on narrated business-crime docs. Proof the faceless format scales. |
| Barely Sociable | Direct | Faceless internet-mystery investigation; the Satoshi video is the tonal template. |
| Fern | Craft | Highest-craft faceless investigative essay on the platform. Study the restraint. |
| ColdFusion | Adjacent | Faceless tech-business documentary; shows the ceiling and the plateau risk. |
| Patrick Boyle | Adjacent | Financial fraud explained dryly and correctly. Good source-handling reference. |
| Bloomberg Originals | Reference | Broadcast-grade investigative finance. Your production target. |
| Dark Docs | Format | Faceless covert-ops reconstruction at high cadence. Structure, not tone. |
| Into the Shadows | Format | Faceless mystery pacing; useful for how to withhold information. |
| Fascinating Horror | Craft | Incident reconstruction with zero sensationalism. The anti-slop model. |

## Direction 2 — *THE MACHINE BEHIND IT*
### The invisible infrastructure that moves money, data and goods

**Premise.** The systems everyone uses and nobody sees: correspondent banking,
stablecoin settlement rails, submarine cables, clearing houses, chip fabs,
container routing, data-centre siting.

**1. Core audience.** 22–45, technical and semi-technical professionals,
engineers, operators, founders, finance. Overlaps heavily with the Wendover /
Asianometry audience — well-monetised and habitual.

**2. Viewer promise.** *You use this every day and you have no idea how it
works. In ten minutes you will.*

**3. Example video topics.**
- The three-day delay in international payments is a choice, not a limit
- Stablecoin settlement quietly became a real payment rail — B2B stablecoin
  volume grew roughly 733% year-over-year to about $226B
- Why Visa settling on-chain matters more than any token price
- Who actually owns the cables carrying your internet
- The single Dutch company every chip depends on
- What happens in the 400 milliseconds a card payment takes
- Why data centres are built where the water is
- The clearing house that could take the whole market down
- The port that breaks when one ship turns sideways
- Why your bank closes on Sunday and USDT does not

**4. Why people would click.** Hidden-mechanism curiosity plus mild
status appeal — this is the content people share to seem informed.

**5. Why people would keep watching.** Nested explanation: each layer reveals a
dependency underneath. Satisfying and low-anxiety, which supports long
watch-time but weaker binge behaviour than crime.

**6. Monetisation potential. High.** Same top CPM band, and a broader,
less cyclical sponsor pool than crypto-specific work.

**7. Sponsorship categories.** B2B SaaS and infrastructure · cloud and CDN ·
fintech and payments · data and observability · semiconductors · logistics
software · developer tools · engineering education · enterprise security.
Arguably the healthiest sponsor market of the five.

**8. Production difficulty. Medium.** Explanation-heavy scripts, high diagram
load. Diagrams are slower to produce well than cinematic shots.

**9. Visual-generation potential. Very high.** Cable ships, fab cleanrooms,
port cranes at night, server halls, undersea trenches, cutaway machine
diagrams. Genuinely beautiful subject matter and Higgsfield's strong suit.

**10. Risk of becoming repetitive AI content. Medium.** The format's failure
mode is well documented — competent narration over stock and animated arrows.
Blends into an established genre rather than reading as AI slop specifically.

**11. What would make it defensible.** Original diagrams as a signature visual
language. First-hand operator sourcing — interviews and documents rather than
re-reported journalism, which is what separates Asianometry from its imitators.
Weakest defensibility of the top three, because the subject matter is public
and several strong channels already own the search results.

## Direction 3 — *MANUFACTURED DESIRE*
### How demand, brands and attention are deliberately engineered

**Premise.** Reverse-engineering persuasion. Why a category exists, why a price
ends in 9, why a launch worked, how a brand manufactured a need that did not
exist. Your GTM advantage pointed directly at camera.

**1. Core audience.** Marketers, founders, product and brand people, agency
staff, ambitious generalists. Small but the most commercially valuable audience
on this list per viewer.

**2. Viewer promise.** *You were sold something today and you did not notice.
Here is the mechanism.*

**3. Example video topics.**
- The category that was invented to sell an existing product
- How a price ending in 9 survived sixty years of evidence
- The launch that worked because it was deliberately hard to buy
- Why every DTC brand looked identical for a decade
- The referral loop that grew faster than the company could serve
- How a luxury brand engineers scarcity it does not have
- The rebrand that cost nine figures and changed one letter
- What "founder-led marketing" is actually selling
- The ad that failed on purpose
- Why your favourite app got worse the moment it worked

**4. Why people would click.** Manipulation-reveal is a strong hook, and the
professional audience clicks for utility as well as curiosity — two motives
stacked.

**5. Why people would keep watching.** Every reveal is immediately applicable.
The viewer is taking notes, which is the strongest retention state there is.

**6. Monetisation potential. High per viewer, moderate in absolute terms.**
Smaller audience ceiling than crime or infrastructure, but the best
sponsor economics on the list — B2B SaaS pays for exactly these people.
Realistic path to a paid tier, course, or advisory pipeline.

**7. Sponsorship categories.** Marketing SaaS · analytics and attribution ·
CRM and email · e-commerce platforms · design tools · AI marketing tooling ·
B2B media and newsletters · recruiting · payments · agency services. Deep,
competitive, and used to paying premium rates.

**8. Production difficulty. Medium.** Cheapest research of the top three —
much is public campaign history — but the hardest to keep honest. Marketing
content drifts toward anecdote and survivorship bias without discipline.

**9. Visual-generation potential. Medium-high.** Product beauty shots,
interface recreations, retail environments, period advertising, brand-system
typography. Real constraint: much of the best material is copyrighted
advertising you cannot legally show at length, forcing recreation.

**10. Risk of becoming repetitive AI content. Medium-high.** This is the most
saturated genre on LinkedIn and Shorts, and it is where AI-generated business
platitudes are thickest. The category is tainted by association.

**11. What would make it defensible.** Original primary research — pricing
teardowns, funnel walk-throughs, ad-library archaeology, practitioner
interviews. Your own operating track record, if you are willing to attach it.
Weakness: the genre rewards the loudest claim, and rigor is a slower growth
curve here than in the other two.

## Direction 4 — *AUTOPSY*
### Rise-and-fall teardowns of internet businesses

**Premise.** The standard business-documentary format executed at a higher
visual grade: what the company was, what it actually sold, the decision that
killed it, what the filings show.

**1. Core audience.** 20–40, business-curious general audience. Large, broad,
well-monetised, but not specialised — the least differentiated audience here.

**2. Viewer promise.** *The real reason it collapsed, from the documents.*

**3. Example video topics.** The startup that was profitable and died anyway ·
the acquisition that destroyed both companies · the app with 50M users and no
business · the fraud hiding in a footnote · the pivot that worked and the pivot
that was a lie · the growth number that was always a ratio · the founder who
was right too early · the company that outlived its market by a decade · the
IPO that priced a story · the metric that stopped being measured.

**4. Why people would click.** Failure is reliable. Named companies bring
built-in search demand.

**5. Why people would keep watching.** Strong three-act structure. Well
understood, which is also the problem — viewers can predict the shape.

**6. Monetisation potential. High.** Business CPM band, broad advertiser fit,
proven at scale by MagnatesMedia.

**7. Sponsorship categories.** Brokerages and investing apps · B2B SaaS ·
business education · productivity tools · recruiting · banking and cards ·
insurance · legal tech.

**8. Production difficulty. Low-medium.** Filings are public and structured.
The easiest of the five to produce consistently.

**9. Visual-generation potential. Medium.** The genre defaults to charts, stock
office footage and logo animation. AI generation lifts it, but the format does
not demand cinema, so the advantage is smaller.

**10. Risk of becoming repetitive AI content. High.** This is the single most
cloned faceless format on YouTube. A large share of low-effort AI channels are
already doing exactly this, which drags the whole category's perceived quality
down — and puts it closest to the inauthentic-content line.

**11. What would make it defensible.** Primary documents others do not read —
filings, dockets, archived pricing pages. A consistent analytical framework
rather than narrative retelling. Honestly: the hardest of the five to defend,
because the moat would have to be craft alone and craft is exactly what AI
tooling is commoditising.

## Direction 5 — *FIELD NOTES FROM 2035*
### Speculative documentary filmed as if the future already happened

**Premise.** Documentaries made from a near-future vantage, looking back at
changes now underway — labour, cities, money, energy, identity. The format that
uses the generation tooling to its absolute ceiling.

**1. Core audience.** 18–35, technology-curious, science-fiction adjacent.
Large and engaged, but younger and lower-intent than the others.

**2. Viewer promise.** *A postcard from the world your current decisions are
building.*

**3. Example video topics.** The last year anyone commuted · the city that
banned private cars and what it cost · the first currency with no country ·
the job that disappeared without anyone noticing · what we did with the empty
offices · the generation that never learned to drive · the year energy became
free and the bill went up · the museum of the smartphone.

**4. Why people would click.** Extremely strong — imagined futures with a
concrete date are inherently clickable, and the thumbnails can be spectacular.

**5. Why people would keep watching.** World-building sustains attention well
if the writing is good. But the viewer knows none of it is verifiable, which
caps investment.

**6. Monetisation potential. Medium.** Younger skew and speculative framing
weaken advertiser appeal. The likely sponsors are consumer AI apps — the most
volatile category on this list.

**7. Sponsorship categories.** Consumer AI apps · creative tools · gaming ·
sci-fi media · energy and EV brands · futures consultancies · education
platforms. Thinner and more cyclical than any other direction here.

**8. Production difficulty. High.** Everything must be generated. Highest
credit burn per finished minute by a wide margin, and no archival fallback
when a shot fails.

**9. Visual-generation potential. Maximum. 10/10.** No copyright constraints,
no reference-accuracy burden, complete creative freedom. This is the only
direction where the tooling is the point rather than the means.

**10. Risk of becoming repetitive AI content. Highest.** Speculative AI video
about the future *is* the archetype of AI slop. You would be competing for
attention inside the exact aesthetic the audience has learned to distrust, and
sitting closest to YouTube's mass-produced-content line.

**11. What would make it defensible.** Genuine writing quality and a coherent
speculative logic where each video builds one consistent world. That is a real
moat — but it is a screenwriting moat, not a research moat, and it does not use
your stated advantages. It also directly contradicts your own rule about
rejecting topics that depend on unsupported claims.

## Recommendation: **THE LEDGER**

Four reasons, in order of weight.

**It is the only direction where your rarest advantage is the moat.** Marketing
knowledge is common among people who would start this channel. Reading a
laundering graph is not. On-chain forensics is a genuine skill barrier, and it
happens to sit on top of a public, timestamped, free dataset that produces
verifiable claims — which is exactly what makes a faceless channel credible
when the audience assumes everything is generated.

**It resolves the central tension of AI-produced content.** The audience's
objection to AI video is not that it looks wrong; it is that it feels like
nobody knew anything. A channel whose product is a source pack inverts that.
The research is unfakeable and the visuals are merely the delivery, which is
also the right side of YouTube's inauthentic-content rules — AI tooling stays
monetisable where the video carries original human value.

**The subject matter is a genuinely renewable resource.** Crypto theft alone
ran $3.4B in 2025 with $2.02B attributed to North Korean actors, and the
historical back catalogue — Mt. Gox, DAO, Ronin, FTX, Bybit — is deep enough to
run for years before touching current events. Fixed format, unlimited input.

**Curiosity and commercial value point the same way here.** In most niches you
trade audience size against audience value. Heist stories pull a general
audience while delivering a finance-grade one to sponsors — and the
security and compliance sponsors that fit this channel buy through bear
markets, which decouples revenue from the crypto cycle.

**The honest case against it.** Crypto-adjacent content carries platform
risk — YouTube treats financial-scam and pump-and-dump content harshly, and an
investigative channel will repeatedly brush against those classifiers. Mitigate
by never promoting an asset, never predicting a price, sourcing every
allegation, and keeping the register forensic rather than accusatory. Second
risk: Coffeezilla is very good and very trusted. The whitespace is real — he is
face-led, US-centric, and works current fraud rather than cinematic
reconstruction of closed cases — but you should not plan to beat him at
live investigation. Do not.

**If you want the safer pick,** take *THE MACHINE BEHIND IT* at 85. It gives up
curiosity pull and defensibility, but it has no platform risk, a broader sponsor
pool and better evergreen decay. It is the better choice if you would rather
build slowly against a proven audience than sharply against a defended one.

### Source-quality notes

Read these caveats before quoting anything in this file on camera.

- **Solid:** Chainalysis figures, FBI attribution, Bybit timeline, YouTube's own
  policy pages. These are primary or near-primary and safe to cite.
- **Directional only:** all CPM and RPM figures. They come from SEO-driven
  industry blogs with no disclosed methodology. Useful for ranking niches
  against each other, not for forecasting revenue.
- **Approximate:** the MagnatesMedia subscriber figure (~1.5M) traces to the
  channel's own course marketing. Every other channel in this file is named
  from domain knowledge without a verified subscriber count — the comparisons
  are editorial judgements about format and positioning, not audience data.
  Verify counts directly before any of this informs a real decision.
- **Not verified:** the stablecoin volume growth figures come from vendor
  marketing content. Do not put them in a script without finding the
  underlying data.
